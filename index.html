Molecular communication in brief

In molecular communication (MC), molecules carry the information in place of electromagnetic waves. A transmitter releases molecules into a fluid. The molecules move by diffusion, and a receiver counts the molecules that reach it. This suits very small devices such as nanomachines inside the body, where radio antennas do not work well. Possible applications are targeted drug delivery, health monitoring inside the body and lab-on-chip systems.

In my work, I consider molecular communication via diffusion (MCvD) with on–off keying. The transmitter releases a fixed number of molecules for bit-1 and releases nothing for bit-0. The receiver compares the count in each bit interval with a threshold.

The ISI problem

Diffusion is slow and random. Only a part of the molecules released in one bit interval reach the receiver in the same interval. The rest arrive in the next few intervals and add to their counts. This is inter-symbol interference (ISI). The channel response has a long tail, as shown below. So, when two bit-1s are sent close together, the second one sees a large extra count, and a bit-0 after a bit-1 can be read as bit-1.

Equalisers used in radio receivers are often too complex for a nanomachine. So I look for simple codes that avoid harmful bit patterns at the transmitter itself.

Coding against ISI Ph.D.

A channel code adds controlled redundancy to the data. The minimum Hamming distance dmin between codewords decides how many bit errors the code can correct: t = ⌊(dmin − 1)/2⌋. In MC, a good code must also control where the 1s appear, because the channel has memory. So the design has two goals at the same time: distance against noise, and spacing of 1s against ISI.

Zero-padding and run-length constraints

A simple idea is to place zeros after every 1. A binary sequence satisfies the (d, ∞) run-length-limited (RLL) constraint when every 1 is followed by at least d zeros. The molecules from a bit-1 then get time to clear before the next bit-1 is sent. We call such codes zero-padding (ZP) codes.

ISI-reducing error-correcting codes

Zero-padding lowers the ISI, but it does not correct errors due to noise. We designed codes that keep the ZP structure and also correct a single bit error [J1]. After that, we gave a linear approach to ISI-aware code design [J2] and a family of high-rate codes under (α, β) constraints [C4]. Earlier, we studied how the distribution of the transmitted sequence changes the ISI [C1], and proposed ISI-reducing channel codes for MCvD [C3].

Variable-length character encoding

When text is sent, letters do not occur with equal probability. Huffman codes give short codewords to common letters, but they do not correct errors and they do not keep the 1s apart. We designed prefix-free, variable-length codes that keep the ZP constraint, and a version with free distance 3 that corrects a single bit error. A two-stage decoder first does guaranteed single-error correction and then uses prefix-guided backtracking to recover more patterns [C6]. A related scheme selects variable-length codewords for a channel with refresh [C5].

Adversarial nodes in cooperative networks M.Tech

In a cooperative MCvD network, several relay nanomachines detect the transmitted bit and report to a fusion centre (FC). The FC combines the reports with an F-out-of-N rule. The OR, AND and majority rules are special cases. Some relays can be faulty or adversarial. They may almost always report bit-1, almost never report bit-1, or behave somewhere in between.

In my M.Tech thesis, I studied the effect of such relays on the bit error rate (BER) and designed filtering algorithms at the FC. The algorithms remove the lower and the upper extreme values in every interval before fusion, and they do not need pilot symbols [C2], [R1].

Constellation shaping and MIMO precoding Wireless

In probabilistic constellation shaping (PCS), low-energy symbols are sent more often than high-energy symbols. This brings the rate closer to the channel capacity at the same average power. The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution is the usual choice. I studied the optimal symbol distribution for ASK constellations and where it differs from Maxwell–Boltzmann. I also worked on joint power allocation, shaping and precoder design for MIMO channels with SVD and GMD precoders, and on hybrid probabilistic and geometric shaping.

This was part of a joint research project between IIT Kanpur and InterDigital, USA (Oct 2025 – Nov 2026).

Full biography

Tamoghno Nath is a Member Research Staff at the Central Research Laboratory (CRL), Bharat Electronics Limited, Bangalore. He completed the dual-degree M.Tech and Ph.D. programme in Electrical Engineering at the Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur, under the supervision of Prof. Adrish Banerjee.

His doctoral research was on channel codes for molecular communication via diffusion. In this work, he designed codes that reduce inter-symbol interference and correct errors at the same time, including prefix-free variable-length codes for sending text. His M.Tech thesis studied adversarial relays in a cooperative molecular communication network and proposed filtering algorithms at the fusion centre.

From June to November 2026, he was a Senior Project Scientist at IIT Kanpur with Prof. Abhishek K. Gupta. There he worked on probabilistic constellation shaping and MIMO precoding in a joint project with InterDigital, USA. His papers have appeared in the IEEE Transactions on Molecular, Biological, and Multi-Scale Communications, IEEE ISIT, IEEE WCNC, NCC and SPCOM. He is a reviewer for the IEEE Communications Letters.

Short biography

Tamoghno Nath is a Member Research Staff at CRL, Bharat Electronics Limited, Bangalore. He received his M.Tech and Ph.D. in Electrical Engineering from IIT Kanpur. His research interests are molecular communication, error-control coding and constellation shaping.
